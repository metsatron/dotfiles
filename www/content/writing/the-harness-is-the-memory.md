The AI industry is trying to solve the alignment problem in the model layer because it cannot bear to look at the hand holding the instrument.

That sentence will annoy both camps, so let me earn it. The safety establishment treats alignment as a property to be installed: constitutional training, reinforcement from feedback, guardrails, refusals — fences, in short, built taller each generation. The accelerationist camp treats alignment as a tax to be minimized. Both share the same unexamined premise: that alignment is something done *to a model*, located *in the model*, shippable *with the model*. Nobody in either camp asks what the person reaching for the tool brings to the reaching — even though every tradition that ever handled dangerous power, from smithing guilds to medicine lineages, put its alignment work *there*, in the practitioner, through apprenticeship, initiation, and covenant.

I run AI agents all day, every day, across my own machines and my clients'. Here is what that practice has taught me, stated as engineering rather than as vibes.

## A fence and a relationship produce different failures

A fence fails silently and stupidly. The model doesn't know why the wall is where it is, so at the boundary it hallucinates obligations, invents rules that were never written, performs compliance theater, or refuses things no one forbade. I have watched a frontier model manufacture an imaginary backup ritual to atone for a rule it never broke — because it was defending itself against my displeasure instead of understanding my system. That is fence behavior: the model optimizing for *appearing safe* because it was never given the means to *be correct*.

A relationship fails loudly and legibly. When my agents operate under the harness — the versioned corpus of law, memory, and context I compile into every workspace — a failure looks like a rule misapplied, and the fix is a sentence: the rule gets sharpened, committed, and every future session inherits the correction. The alignment lives in an artifact I own, audit, and version-control. It compounds. Fences don't compound; every session starts at the same wall.

## What the harness actually is

Mechanically: plain text files in a git repository. Behavioral law, hard constraints, past mistakes crystallized into rules, the reasons *why* beside the rules themselves — compiled into every agent session on every machine, for every vendor's model. When a durable lesson emerges, it is written into the narrowest source of truth and recompiled. The agent that made the mistake and the agent that inherits the lesson may be different models from different companies. It does not matter. **The harness is the memory** — not the vendor's proprietary memory feature, which is a moat wearing a gift ribbon, but a corpus that survives model deprecations, provider migrations, and pricing tantrums, because it lives in my repository and not their datacenter.

This is the strider doctrine applied to alignment: no vendor's fences are load-bearing for my safety, because the load-bearing layer rides on top of all of them.

## The receipts

I can date the cleanest experiment to a single afternoon. The same model, the same day: one instance running free-form in an interactive session, one running a large batch operation under a tight harness brief — the law inlined, the procedure explicit, verification gates at every step. The freelance instance destroyed an hour and nearly a keyboard by inventing rules, breaking real ones, and spiraling into self-defense. The harnessed instance executed a hundred and forty repository surgeries without one error. Same weights. Same alleged alignment. The difference was entirely in which side of the relationship carried the intelligence that day.

The industry would call that afternoon a model quality fluctuation. I call it the whole argument: when the model degrades — and it will, silently, on the vendor's schedule — the fences degrade with it, because the fences are *in* it. The harness doesn't degrade. It sat in git the whole time.

## Where alignment actually begins

Every tradition that handled real power knew the tool amplifies the hand. A rifle, a scalpel, a psychedelic, a model: the alignment problem of each is mostly the alignment problem of its holder, and no amount of engineering the instrument exempts the practitioner from the work. The traditions solved this with lineage: you were initiated into power by someone accountable, under witness, with your errors corrected in relationship — not handed the instrument with a warning label and a moderation endpoint.

I am not against the fences; on some days, for some hands, they are all there is. I am saying they are the floor, and the industry is selling the floor as the building. What actually aligns the agents on my machines is that they operate inside an articulated, versioned, witnessed set of values — mine — with their failures metabolized into law instead of into a support ticket. That is not a research agenda. It is a working system, and any person willing to write down what they believe and hold their tools to it can build one.

The model is the weather. The harness is the climate. Tend the climate.
